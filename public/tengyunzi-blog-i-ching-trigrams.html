The Eight Trigrams Explained

The eight trigrams are the alphabet of the I Ching. Learn these eight three-line figures and the entire system of sixty-four hexagrams opens up, because every hexagram is simply two trigrams stacked together.

  • Humanity
  • I Ching
  • 8 min read

Before you can read the sixty-four hexagrams, it helps enormously to know the eight trigrams they are built from. Called the bagua, these three-line figures are the core vocabulary of the whole I Ching — and they connect directly to the same forces that run through Chinese metaphysics as a whole.

Each trigram is a small image of a natural force — heaven, earth, thunder, water, mountain, wind, fire, lake — and a quality of movement. Together they map the basic situations energy can take.

How a Trigram Is Built

A trigram is three lines stacked one above another, and each line is either yang (solid) or yin (broken). With three positions and two possibilities each, there are exactly eight combinations — no more, no fewer. That is where the eight comes from.

This is the same yin and yang logic that underlies all of Chinese thought, applied at the smallest scale. Read from the bottom line up, the pattern of solid and broken lines gives each trigram its character.

The Eight Trigrams

Each trigram carries a natural image, a family role, and a quality. These associations are worth knowing because they colour every hexagram the trigram appears in.

Reading a Trigram's Lines

Within each trigram, the position of the single odd line often carries its meaning. Kan (Water), for example, is one solid line held between two broken ones — strength hidden in the middle, which is why it suggests depth and danger both. Li (Fire) is the mirror image, one broken line between two solid ones — a bright surface with emptiness at the core, hence clarity that depends on what it clings to. Learning to see these small patterns turns the trigrams from symbols to memorise into images you can actually read.

How Trigrams Build Hexagrams

Stack any two trigrams — an upper and a lower — and you get a six-line hexagram. Eight trigrams paired in every combination give exactly sixty-four hexagrams, the full set of the I Ching. This is why the trigrams are the key: learn eight images and you can read the meaning of any hexagram as a relationship between its two halves.

For example, water over fire, or fire over water, are very different situations — the order of the trigrams matters, and reading a hexagram often begins by noticing which force sits above which.

The Bagua Beyond the I Ching

The eight trigrams travel well beyond the I Ching. The same bagua appears in feng shui, where its eight directions map onto a space, and in other branches of Chinese metaphysics. Recognising the trigrams gives you a shared key across several traditions at once.

For the purposes of the I Ching, though, their job is simple: they are the building blocks. Knowing them turns hexagram-reading from memorisation into understanding.

From Trigrams to Reading

Once the eight trigrams feel familiar, the sixty-four hexagrams stop looking like a wall of symbols and start reading as combinations you can reason about. The natural next step is the hexagrams themselves, and then using the I Ching for a decision.

As always, the aim is reflection, not prophecy — the trigrams give you a richer language for thinking a situation through, and the choice stays yours.

Eight three-line figures, each a force of nature. Learn the trigrams and the sixty-four hexagrams become combinations you can read, not symbols you must memorise.
