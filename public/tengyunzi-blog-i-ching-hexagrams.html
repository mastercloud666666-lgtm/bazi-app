The 64 Hexagrams Explained

The sixty-four hexagrams are the heart of the I Ching — sixty-four six-line figures, each with a short chapter, that between them describe the full range of situations a person can face. This is how to read one.

  • Humanity
  • I Ching
  • 9 min read

If the eight trigrams are the alphabet of the I Ching, the sixty-four hexagrams are its words. Each is a complete image of a situation, paired with a short text offering a considered perspective on it. Learning to read a hexagram is what turns the I Ching from a curiosity into a usable tool.

You do not need to memorise all sixty-four. You need to understand how one is built and how to read it, and the rest becomes a matter of looking things up thoughtfully.

Six Lines, One Situation

A hexagram is six lines stacked vertically, each either yang (solid) or yin (broken), read from the bottom up. Six positions with two possibilities each give sixty-four combinations — the complete set. Every one has a name, a number in the traditional sequence, and a short chapter of text.

The genius of the system is coverage: between them, the sixty-four hexagrams map the archetypal situations of a life — beginning and difficulty, waiting, conflict, union, abundance, decline, return, and so on. Whatever you are facing has a shape, and one hexagram describes it.

Reading the Two Trigrams

The fastest way into a hexagram is to split it into its two halves: the lower three lines (the inner trigram) and the upper three (the outer trigram). Each is one of the eight trigrams, and the hexagram's meaning often flows directly from their relationship.

Water sitting over Fire, for instance, is a different situation from Fire over Water. Reading which force is above and which below, and how they interact, gives you the gist of a hexagram before you even reach the text.

The Parts of a Hexagram Reading

Each hexagram's chapter has a few standard parts, and knowing them makes any translation easier to use.

PartWhat it gives you
The JudgmentAn overall reading of the situation
The ImageA natural picture and the attitude it suggests
The LinesSpecific notes for each of the six positions

Changing Lines and the Second Hexagram

A hexagram is rarely static. When you cast one, some lines may be "changing" — old yin or old yang — and these carry the most pointed guidance for your moment. When the changing lines flip to their opposite, they form a second hexagram.

That pair tells a small story: the first hexagram is the situation now, the second is where it tends if the change plays out. This movement is the point of the Book of Changes. The method for casting and reading these is covered in how to use the I Ching for a decision.

A Few Well-Known Hexagrams

Some hexagrams are worth knowing early because they anchor the whole sequence. The first is Qian, The Creative — six solid lines, pure yang, the image of Heaven and strong initiating energy. The second is Kun, The Receptive — six broken lines, pure yin, the image of Earth and yielding support. These two poles frame everything between them. A little further in, Meng (Youthful Folly) speaks to learning and inexperience, while Sheng (Pushing Upward) describes steady, gradual progress. You do not need them memorised, but recognising a few gives you landmarks in the sixty-four.

Using the Hexagrams Well

Read a hexagram as a mirror, not a verdict. Its job is to describe your situation in a way that interrupts your assumptions and makes you look more clearly — recognition first, guidance second. Often the reframing is the whole value.

Held this way, the sixty-four hexagrams are one of the oldest and richest maps of human situations ever compiled: a language for thinking a hard moment through, with the decision always remaining yours.

Sixty-four situations, each a hexagram. Read the two trigrams, note the changing lines, and treat the text as a mirror for thinking — never as a fixed verdict.
