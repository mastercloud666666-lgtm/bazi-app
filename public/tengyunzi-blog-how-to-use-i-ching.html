How to Use the I Ching for a Decision

Reading about the I Ching is one thing; using it on a real question is another. This is the practical method — how to frame a question, cast a hexagram, and read the result as a thinking tool rather than a verdict.

  • Humanity
  • I Ching
  • 9 min read

The I Ching earns its keep when you bring it a genuine decision — a choice where the facts alone have not settled the matter. The process is simple and takes minutes, but the quality of what you get out depends almost entirely on one thing: the question you put in.

This guide walks the full loop the way a careful practitioner does it: frame, cast, read, and interpret. Hold onto the frame throughout — you are using the I Ching to think, not to be told what to do.

Step 1 — Frame the Question

Everything depends on this step. A vague question ("What will happen with my career?") produces a vague, unusable reading. A good question is specific, present-tense, and about your own action: "What is the wise approach to taking this new role?" or "What should I understand before ending this partnership?"

Write the question down before you cast. The act of writing forces the fog into words, and half the value of the I Ching is often delivered right here, before a single coin is thrown. Avoid yes/no questions; ask for perspective, not permission.

Step 2 — Cast the Hexagram

The traditional method uses yarrow stalks; the common modern method uses three coins, which is quicker and perfectly valid. You toss the three coins six times, building the hexagram from the bottom line up. Assign heads a value of 3 and tails a value of 2, and add the three coins for each throw.

Coin totalLineMeaning
6Old yin (broken, changing)Yin turning to yang
7Young yang (solid)Stable yang
8Young yin (broken)Stable yin
9Old yang (solid, changing)Yang turning to yin

Step 3 — Read the Hexagram

Six throws give you six lines and therefore one of the 64 hexagrams. Look it up and read its main text — the judgment and the image. Resist the urge to skim for a verdict; read it as a description of the situation you are in and the posture it recommends.

The first reading is about recognition. Does the hexagram's picture of the situation ring true? Often it reframes the question in a way you had not considered, and that reframing is the useful part — more than any single phrase in the text.

Step 4 — Read the Changing Lines

If any throw totalled 6 or 9, that line is "changing." Read the specific text for each changing line — these speak most directly to your moment — and then note the second hexagram formed when the changing lines flip to their opposite.

The pair tells a small story: the first hexagram is the situation now, the second is where it is tending if the change plays out. This movement from one to the other is the heart of the I Ching as a Book of Changes, and it is often where the real guidance lives.

Step 5 — Interpret as Reflection

Now close the loop honestly. The reading has given you a considered outside perspective; the decision is still yours to make. Sit with the question: given this reframing, what does a measured response look like? What were you avoiding that the hexagram named?

Do not outsource the choice to the coins. Use the I Ching the way you would use a wise, impartial friend — to think more clearly, not to be relieved of responsibility. If you want help framing and reading a real question, Tengyunzi's decision companion is built for exactly this.

The quality of an I Ching reading is set by the quality of the question. Ask for perspective on your own action, never for a yes or a no.
